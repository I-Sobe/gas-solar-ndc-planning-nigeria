## The divergence, restated

This model anchors gas-fired generation on **observed deliverability**:
89.27 TWh_th/yr, derived from NERC's 2024 generation of 25.62 TWh_e at
a measured fleet efficiency of 0.287, cross-checked against reported
gas-to-power volumes of 800–860 MMscf/d [TASK 77 A.1: no source exists for this range, register L130; it is not a sourced cross-check].

NIRP's trajectory runs **22.3 TWh_e (2024) → 49.9 (2030) → 88.0 (2035)
→ 64.5 (2045)**.

The two agree for 2024 and diverge by ~3.4x by 2035. **The entire
divergence is about how fast midstream delivery improves.**

## Line 1 — the pipeline programme's delivery against its own plan

**AKK (Ajaokuta–Kaduna–Kano), $2.8bn, 614 km, 40-inch.**

<cite index="135-1">The project was conceived by NNPC in 2008, with the tender process beginning in July 2013</cite>. <cite index="135-1">Construction was flagged off in 2020 on a 24-month schedule — June 2020 to June 2022</cite>.

The delivery record against that schedule:

| target announced | source | outcome |
|---|---|---|
| June 2022 | original 24-month schedule | missed |
| December 2023 | NNPC EVP Upstream, Nov 2023 | missed |
| Q1 2025 | NNPC GCEO Kyari | missed |
| Q4 2025 | subsequent revision | missed |
| July 2026 | NUPRC, April 2026 | see below |

<cite index="132-1">Earlier deadlines of 2023 and the final quarter of 2025 were both missed, and commissioning was pushed to 2026</cite>. As of NNPC's July 2026 monthly report, <cite index="139-1">AKK stands at 95 per cent complete, with construction and installation "at an advanced stage to deliver early gas to Abuja in 2026"</cite> — no capacity or commissioning date given.

**Six years from construction start, against a 24-month schedule, with
no gas delivered.**

**OB3 (Obiafu–Obrikom–Oben), $700m, 2.4 bcf/d design capacity.**

<cite index="139-1">Started in 2016, the project missed several completion deadlines before reaching 100 per cent, with pre-commissioning completed in readiness for first gas in August 2026</cite>. **Ten years from start to first gas.**

**The argument.** NIRP's trajectory is downstream of exactly these
projects. A programme whose two flagship pipelines took six and ten
years against schedules of two and (implicitly) fewer is a programme
whose delivery assumption is load-bearing and undeclared. **NIRP is
not wrong to assume its own programme succeeds — but it does not state
that it is assuming it**, and this model declines to.

## Line 2 — the payment chain, and why this is commercial not geological

This is the strongest line, and it locates the constraint where this
thesis already argues it sits: Nigeria holds 200+ TCF of proved
reserves, so physical depletion is unavailable to either side.

**The mechanism, documented end to end.**
<cite index="127-1">Electricity generated by GenCos is purchased through the market and supplied to DisCos, which collect revenue from customers; that revenue should flow back through the chain, allowing GenCos to pay gas suppliers. DisCos and NBET both remit less than 100 per cent of what is owed each month, leaving generation companies with growing unpaid invoices — and as shortfalls accumulate, GenCos cannot pay gas suppliers</cite>.

**The magnitudes, 2025 and 2026:**

| quantity | figure |
|---|---|
| GenCo invoices issued, 2025 | ₦3.16tn |
| Cash actually remitted | ₦1.17tn |
| **Settlement rate** | **39.25%** |
| Shortfall | ₦1.92tn |
| Of which unpaid subsidy obligations | ₦1.85tn (96%) |
| Paid against the shortfall | ₦71.49bn (3.7%) |
| Sector debt, Feb 2026 | ₦6.8tn |
| Owed to gas suppliers | ~₦3.3tn |
| Projected by Dec 2026 | ₦8.8tn |

<cite index="131-1">GenCos issued invoices totalling ₦3.16 trillion in 2025; cumulative payments were ₦1.24 trillion and actual cash remittances ₦1.17 trillion — a settlement rate of 39.25 per cent</cite>. <cite index="131-1">Of the ₦1.92 trillion owed, ₦1.85 trillion — over 96 per cent — was unpaid subsidy obligations rather than DisCo market shortfall</cite>.

**The monthly run-rate is the number that matters for a forward
trajectory.** <cite index="129-1">Of every ₦280 billion in monthly invoices, only 35 per cent is paid, leaving roughly ₦200 billion accumulating every month; ₦2.4 trillion was added in 2025 alone</cite>.

**And the physical consequence is documented, not inferred.**
<cite index="125-1">Ibom Power, a 190 MW thermal plant, has produced no electricity since 2025 after its gas supplier suspended deliveries over unpaid bills</cite>. <cite index="125-1">In March 2026, 16 of Nigeria's 33 power plants were offline, reducing available capacity to 3,700–4,000 MW against a typical 4,000–5,500 MW</cite>.

The APGC chief executive states the mechanism directly:
<cite index="129-1">gas is unavailable because suppliers require payment up front before delivering into the pipe</cite>.

**The argument.** If the binding constraint is payment rather than
pipeline, **midstream capacity alone does not lift it.** NIRP's
trajectory is premised on commissioning capacity; the observed
constraint is that existing capacity is not being paid for. A
trajectory that assumes delivery therefore assumes a reform of the
payment chain, not only an infrastructure programme — and it does not
say so.

## Line 3 — the quantitative test: even full delivery does not reach NIRP

**This is the strongest single argument and it is arithmetic.**

<cite index="140-1">NNPC states that completion of OB3 and AKK would, in the near term, unlock over 500 million standard cubic feet per day of additional gas supply for the domestic market</cite>.

Converting at the model's own basis (1,036 Btu/scf HHV):

    500 MMscf/d x 365 d          = 182,500 MMscf/yr
    x 1,036 Btu/scf              = 1.891e14 Btu/yr
    / 3.412e12 Btu per TWh_th    = 55.4 TWh_th/yr

**So both flagship pipelines, fully delivering, add ~55 TWh_th/yr — to
the domestic market as a whole, not to power alone.**

Against what NIRP's 2035 figure requires:

| | TWh_e | implied TWh_th |
|---|---|---|
| NIRP 2035 target | 88.0 | **306.6** at fleet η 0.287 |
| | | **191.3** at retrofit η 0.46 |
| This model's current deliverability | 25.6 | 89.27 |
| **Gap to close** | | **102 to 217 TWh_th/yr** |

**OB3 and AKK together close 25% to 54% of that gap — and only if
every molecule went to power**, which it will not: the 500 MMscf/d is
explicitly for the domestic market including <cite index="140-1">manufacturing and exports</cite>.

**Conclusion, stated for the chapter:** Nigeria's two flagship gas
transmission projects, both now at or near completion after six and
ten years respectively, would at full delivery supply less than half
the additional thermal input NIRP's 2035 generation target requires —
before any allocation to industry. The divergence between this model
and NIRP is therefore not a modelling disagreement about cost. **It is
a disagreement about whether the midstream programme can deliver a
quantity that its own two largest components do not reach.**

## The concession this section must still make

**State plainly** that this model assumes deliverability does not
materially improve, that this assumption generates the solar residual
and the entire chain of results, and that it is anchored to **observed
constrained throughput used as a capability parameter** — the same
category of inference this thesis criticises in three other parameters
(the 23.08 TWh demand base, the peak-truncated load factor, the
supply-series growth rate).

> It is retained because the alternative — assuming the midstream
> programme delivers — is the assumption NIRP makes and does not
> declare, and because the direction of the resulting bias is knowable:
> if deliverability improves faster than assumed, this model overstates
> the solar requirement and therefore overstates the financing gap. The
> thesis's central claim, that capital availability rather than target
> stringency binds, would weaken but not reverse, since the capital
> constraint applies to whatever is built.

**And note what Line 3 does to that concession.** The arithmetic gives
an upper bound on how fast deliverability could improve even under
full programme success. The bias direction is not merely knowable —
it is bounded.

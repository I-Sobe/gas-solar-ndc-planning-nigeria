**Gas Deliverability and the Financing of Power-Sector Decarbonization in Nigeria: Energy-Security Trade-offs in a Capacity-Expansion Model.**

# Abstract {#abstract-1}

Nigeria’s grid must expand under a gas deliverability constraint, a finite supply of concessional capital and a decarbonization commitment drawn around grid supply, and a least-cost plan optimized against a single demand projection meets that projection exactly while carrying no capacity-adequacy margin. This thesis develops a deterministic linear capacity-expansion model of the Nigerian power system, solved over 2025 to 2054 and reported to 2045, in which the division of each vintage’s capital between a concessional and a commercial tranche is a decision variable bounded by a finite concessional envelope and disciplined by a revenue-adequacy condition on privately financed capacity, a formulation no study in the reviewed sub-Saharan African literature contains. Among the parameter families describing attainable states of the system, after gas fleet availability and the cost of operation, the largest determinants are gas deliverability and the conversion of existing open-cycle plant. The value of relieving the gas deliverability constraint is substantial in the absence of an emissions commitment, and falls to zero under one in every supply regime except where gas supply binds independently of the cap. The value of the service-financed channel is conditional on the public capital budget binding, and where it binds the same optimum is reached on 60.0 to 62.8 per cent less public capital. Tightening a commitment drawn at the grid boundary past a measured turning point raises national emissions, as load displaced to diesel self-generation exceeds the abatement achieved on the grid.

**Keywords:** Gas Deliverability, Energy Security, Energy System, Power System, Project Finance

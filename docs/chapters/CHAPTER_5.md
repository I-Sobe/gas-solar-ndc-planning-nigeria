# Chapter 5 — Discussion

## 5.1 Scope and organisation

Chapter 4 reports what the model measures. This chapter interprets those measurements: what they imply for the design of policy and financing instruments in Nigeria’s power sector, how they stand against the literature reviewed in Chapter 2, what conditions their reading, and what work follows from them.

Results are not restated here. Each interpretation names the result it rests on by cross-reference and proceeds from it. Where a figure is necessary to the interpretation it appears once.

Two conditions established in Chapter 3 govern every section below and are stated here rather than at each result they qualify. The first concerns gas fleet availability. Results turning on feasibility or on frontier position are invariant to the choice between the availability arms, because under unconstrained gas build a lower availability is answered by additional capacity; results turning on dual values are not (§3.7.5, §4.1.4). The second concerns the emissions constraint. Every cap-compliance statement in this thesis is a statement about the grid boundary, because the cap binds grid-supplied emissions and self-generation lies outside it and is unbounded (§3.7.6, §4.4.1).

The limitations discussed in §5.8 are drawn from §3.7 and from the limitations register reproduced as Appendix B. They are not restated at each result, with one exception: where a limitation changes the direction in which a specific interpretation should be read, it is named at that interpretation and treated again in §5.8.

## 5.2 A gas-determined pathway under a renewables-framed policy

### 5.2.1 The distinction between what is built and what determines cost

The determinants of system cost reported in §4.2 are predominantly properties of the gas system: the condition of the existing fleet, the operations permitted on it, the schedule on which it leaves service, the quantity of fuel reaching it, and the efficiency with which that fuel is converted. Nine of the fourteen ranked families are gas-side, and no solar-side parameter enters the ranked table at any position, with no policy in force or under a binding emissions commitment (§4.2.3, §4.2.6).

This is not a finding that solar capacity is incidental to the pathway. Solar is the largest single line in the capital programme, and the capacity the central solution builds by 2045 is substantially solar (§4.1.2, §4.3.4). The finding concerns a different quantity. What the plan builds and what determines what the plan costs are distinct, and in this system they fall on opposite sides of the fuel supply. The quantity of solar the model builds is largely insensitive to the parameters that move system cost; the cost of the system is set by whether the existing gas fleet can run, by how much gas reaches it, and by the efficiency with which it converts that gas. Uncertainty about solar does not propagate into cost in this formulation. Uncertainty about the gas system does, and at a scale no other family approaches.

The asymmetry conditions how a decarbonisation policy framed around renewable deployment should be read. Instruments addressed to the rate at which renewable capacity is procured act on the component of the plan whose parameters do not determine its cost. The parameters that do determine it are midstream supply and asset-condition quantities, for which such instruments provide no mechanism. The result does not argue against renewable procurement, which the least-cost plan undertakes at scale without being instructed to. It locates the questions on which the cost of the pathway turns elsewhere than where the policy architecture directs its instruments.

The ranking under a binding commitment sharpens this rather than qualifying it. Where the emissions constraint binds, the families that rise are the availability of new gas capacity and the efficiency with which combined-cycle plant converts fuel, the latter moving from fourteenth position to fourth (§4.2.6). The system meets a tightening commitment by converting fuel more efficiently inside the boundary rather than by displacing demand beyond it (§4.4.2). A commitment on grid emissions operates, in this system, as an instrument acting on the gas fleet.

One condition attaches to the absence of solar-side parameters. Membership of the ranked table is declared rather than inferred (§4.2.2): the table reports the families the scenario definitions contain. The absence is an absence within a declared set, and the stronger proposition — that no solar-side quantity could enter it — is not one this study establishes.

### 5.2.2 Standing of the conversion programme

Conversion of open-cycle to combined-cycle plant is treated in this thesis as a fuel-efficiency instrument rather than a capacity instrument (§1.1.3). Its standing changed when capacity added within the model was assigned its own conversion efficiency in place of a single fleet-wide rate (§4.1.5): it falls from second position to fourth, and its span falls by approximately two-fifths (§4.2.3).

The mechanism is a change in the alternative against which conversion is valued. Under a single fleet-wide efficiency, converting an existing unit was the only route by which the system could obtain efficient gas generation, and the measured value of a conversion programme carried the whole of that efficiency gain. Once new build earns the combined-cycle efficiency in its own right, conversion competes against new combined-cycle capacity, and what it is worth is the difference between converting an existing unit and building a new one rather than the difference between an efficient unit and the fleet as it stands.

Two implications follow. For appraisal, a conversion programme evaluated against the status quo fleet will be assigned a value this formulation does not support once the alternative of efficient new build is admitted into the comparison. For capital planning, conversion is not a programme-reducing instrument. Against the no-conversion arm it raises capital mobilised over the reporting window by ⟦1.9⟧ per cent, and adds solar and storage capacity rather than displacing them. Conversion reduces the cost of the pathway while enlarging the capital programme that has to be raised to deliver it, and these are different quantities answering to different institutions (§5.3.3).

The composition of the capital programme carries a further implication, for the financing instrument rather than the asset. The programme divides in approximately equal shares between solar capacity, new gas capacity and the conversion of existing gas capacity (§4.3.4), while the concessional envelope is restricted to renewable capital. That restriction is a declared modelling assumption and not a rule of the facility whose scale the envelope takes (§1.5.2, §3.4.3). Within the model as specified, an instrument so restricted reaches approximately half of the capital a least-cost pathway requires, and the two thirds it does not reach are the two thirds on which the cost of the pathway turns. Whether a concessional dollar is better deployed on gas capacity is a question this formulation cannot answer, and the direction of that inability is stated in §3.4.3.

### 5.2.3 The dominant parameter and the measurement it implies

Gas fleet availability is the largest determinant among the parameters that change the capacity decisions, and the variance decomposition assigns it approximately nineteen twentieths of the variance in both system cost and capital mobilised (§4.2.7). Its span runs between an assumed upper endpoint and a measured lower one (§3.7.5).

The decomposition supports a conditional statement rather than an unconditional one. Conditional on fleet availability being unknown between its two declared bounds, it accounts for approximately nine-tenths of the uncertainty in both quantities, and no other parameter approaches it. The index is in part a property of the width of that interval, and the interval is wide because no Nigerian fuel-agnostic estimate could be constructed: two attempts were made and neither succeeded (§3.7.5). The decomposition does not establish that availability matters more than other physical quantities; it quantifies what not knowing it costs.

The same analysis contains its own control. Fleet thermal efficiency is mechanically central to the formulation, converting fuel to electricity and, through the electrical emission factor, setting the emissions intensity of grid supply. It contributes almost nothing to output variance, because its band is narrow, and its band is narrow because the quantity was measured. A well-identified parameter contributes little to output uncertainty even where it is mechanically central. That contrast is the argument for measurement expressed as a result rather than as a principle, and it is available only from a variance-based decomposition: a one-at-a-time sensitivity establishes that availability is the largest lever without establishing that the remaining parameters are nearly irrelevant beside it.

Both readings indicate the same action. The highest-value measurement available to Nigerian power-system planning, on the evidence of this model, is a fuel-agnostic availability factor for the existing gas fleet: one separating mechanical and atmospheric availability from feedstock unavailability, so that fuel scarcity is not counted twice against a system whose gas balance already represents it. The quantity is not a modelling construct. It is an operating statistic of a fleet whose outages are recorded, and its construction is an exercise in classification and disclosure rather than in estimation.

The return on that measurement extends beyond the ranking. The model sets the firm-capacity credit for gas equal to the gas availability factor, and the two are different quantities (§3.7.4). A sourced fuel-agnostic factor would therefore also discharge the condition under which the adequacy results of §4.5 are read as optimistic. One measurement bears on the largest determinant of cost and on the credibility of the adequacy margin at the same time.

## 5.3 Determination of capital structure

### 5.3.1 Conditions under which the determination is informative

The division of each vintage’s capital between concessional and commercial sources is determined within the model, and determining it is worth between ⟦0.58⟧ and ⟦3.46⟧ per cent of system cost, rising with the size of the concessional facility (§4.3.2). The comparison is against the same model with the division supplied as data. At the facility’s actual size, that figure is ⟦61⟧ per cent of what the facility is worth in total: most of the value of concessional finance lies in where it is placed rather than in how much of it there is.

The determination is uninformative only where a supplied rule happens to reproduce the model’s own allocation. At the facility’s actual size the model places the whole of it in a single vintage, and any rule that finds that vintage is as good as the model; at ten times the size the model spreads it across four vintages at full share and several more in part, and no simple rule reproduces that (§4.3.3). The condition is therefore the richness of the allocation problem, not the scarcity of public capital: the measurement is taken where no public capital budget binds at all, and the value is positive throughout.

This bears on the gap identified in Chapter 2. The reviewed corpus supplies the cost of capital as an input in sixty-five of seventy-five studies and determines it in none (§2.5.1, §2.7.1). A model whose capital charge is a fixed scalar cannot represent a concessional facility of finite size, and therefore cannot represent the allocation decision that carries the majority of that facility’s value. What is foreclosed by supplying the rate is not a marginal refinement but the question of where scarce concessional capital should go.

A separate conditionality attaches to the financing channel rather than to the division. The value of raising capacity through a service-financed channel instead of on the public account is zero where the public capital budget does not bind and large where it does, with no configuration between the two (§4.3.4). That separation is exact and is a property of the formulation: where the budget does not bind the two sources are perfect substitutes at the margin and the division between them lies on a flat face. The two results should not be read as one. The first varies continuously with the size of a resource limit; the second switches on whether a budget constraint binds.

### 5.3.2 Effect where the budget binds, and what the financing channel buys

Where the public capital budget binds, financing capacity through the service-financed channel rather than on the public account reduces the public capital required to reach the same optimum by ⟦60.0⟧ to ⟦62.8⟧ per cent (§4.3.4). Both regimes reach an identical plan: the same system cost, the same solar capacity and the same storage. What differs is the quantity of public capital consumed in reaching it. This is a property of the channel and not of determining the division within a vintage, which §5.3.1 treats separately.

The composition of that reduction requires care, and the measurement distinguishes two things a policy reader would otherwise conflate. The service-financed channel and the concessional envelope act on the same constraint by different routes: the envelope relaxes the quantity of concessional capital available, while the service-financed channel removes capital expenditure from the public account by transferring it to a tariff-funded counterparty. Under the current commitment the measured value of the service-financed channel equals the cost of the public envelope to the cent, which is to say that the channel’s entire value at that commitment is the value of the envelope it displaces. The channel buys nothing against the emissions cap itself.

The policy reading follows directly. A service-based or private-capital-mobilisation structure is not an abatement instrument. It does not make a commitment cheaper to meet in resource terms, and it does not change what the system builds. It changes which balance sheet carries the capital, and it is worth precisely the public capital it relieves. Where no public capital constraint binds, it is worth approximately nothing (§1.5.3, §4.3.2). Where one binds, it is worth the cost of relieving it by other means.

Two conditions qualify the magnitude. The mechanism the channel represents is generic private capital mobilisation, comprising budget decoupling together with a revenue-adequacy test, and nothing in the formulation is specific to a service contract as distinct from any other structure moving capital off the public balance sheet and subjecting it to such a test (§1.5.2, §3.4.6). And the division between publicly and service-financed solar is not identified by the formulation: the quantity of solar that should be raised through the channel lies on a flat face and is bounded rather than located (§1.6.7, §4.3.2). The result concerns the budgetary consequence of the channel, not an optimal financing structure.

### 5.3.3 Scale of concessional finance against the capital programme

The concessional envelope used in this study is approximately one tenth of the capital mobilised over the reporting window, and the full span from a zero envelope to ten times the base scale moves system cost by approximately one fifth of that capital programme (§4.2.3, §4.2.4). Across that span the terminal plan is identical in solar, gas and storage capacity; what moves is the timing of investment, with approximately one seventh of the solar build displaced in time at the two largest scales (§4.2.3).

Three statements follow, and they are distinct.

A concessional facility at this scale does not set the marginal cost of capital. The envelope is drawn in full and exhausted in a single vintage at the central configuration (§4.1.2), so the marginal dollar is commercial everywhere, and an inframarginal quantity of cheap capital changes the total bill rather than the choice. The instrument changes who pays for the programme; at this scale it does not change what the programme is.

The quantity it should be measured against is the capital programme, not system cost. A planner asking what the pathway costs reads the system-cost denominator; an institution allocating concessional capital acts on the capital programme, and against that denominator the financing parameters are approximately three times larger in relative terms (§4.1.2, §4.2.4). Reporting the financing contribution only against system cost understates it for the institution most likely to act on it.

The scale mismatch is larger than the envelope-to-programme comparison suggests. The implied midstream investment required to deliver the gas volumes the planning chain assumes exceeds this model’s entire power-sector capital programme by a factor of between one and eight. The mismatch is therefore not between concessional finance and a midstream cost alone; it is between concessional finance as currently sized and Nigerian energy capital formation taken as a whole.

## 5.4 The boundary of a commitment and the financing of compliance

### 5.4.1 The determinants of emissions outside the boundary

Two determinants operate on the quantity of emissions falling outside the grid commitment boundary, and they act under different commitments. The commitment itself displaces demand where it binds hard enough; the capital constraint displaces demand where it prevents the capacity that would meet the commitment from being built (§4.4.2). Three findings derived under the preceding specification attributed the whole of the displacement to the capital constraint, and all three are reversed (§4.1.5).

Under the conditional arm of the current commitment, the cap displaces demand on its own account. With no capital budget in force the central arm places ⟦12.76⟧ per cent of its emissions outside the boundary over the reporting window, and ⟦55⟧ of the ⟦58⟧ declared arms exceed the ⟦10⟧ per cent threshold. Adding a public capital budget at the tier named tight changes that figure to ⟦12.65⟧ per cent and the count not at all. Where the commitment is stringent enough to bind on the capacity the system can physically build, the financing constraint adds little that the cap has not already done.

Under the unconditional arm the position reverses. With no capital budget the central arm places ⟦0.14⟧ per cent outside the boundary and ⟦2⟧ of ⟦58⟧ arms exceed the threshold; with the tight budget the central figure rises to ⟦3.91⟧ per cent, and across collection rates the tight tier runs between ⟦3.9⟧ and ⟦8.8⟧ per cent against between ⟦0.0⟧ and ⟦0.14⟧ per cent at the tier named moderate (§4.4.2). Here the capital constraint is the determinant, and it raises the out-of-boundary share by between one and two orders of magnitude.

The basis on which the share is taken matters as much as the configuration, and the two commitments behave oppositely. The conditional cap’s displacement falls in the years before 2038, so the horizon-cumulative share dilutes it: ⟦7⟧ of ⟦58⟧ arms flag over 2025–2054 against ⟦55⟧ over the reporting window. The unconditional cap’s displacement under a tight budget falls after 2043, so the horizon sum exaggerates it: ⟦57⟧ of ⟦58⟧ against ⟦2⟧. Every figure above is taken over the reporting window the thesis reports against (§1.6.3). The diagnostic itself sums over the whole solved horizon, and a reader comparing these figures against a stored output will find the other basis (§3.5.6, Appendix B).

The mechanism is the same in both cases and is specific. The cap constrains grid emissions; the budget constrains the capacity that can be built inside the grid boundary; and self-generation is an unbounded, unregulated and less efficient backstop outside both (§3.3.7, §3.7.6). A system permitted and able to build its way into compliance does so. A system prevented from doing so, whether by the stringency of the commitment or by the capital available to meet it, meets the cap on paper by shedding load to plant the cap does not reach.

### 5.4.2 Sequencing of climate finance against emissions commitments

The implication for the sequencing of climate finance and emissions commitments is direct, and it is the principal policy conclusion of this study.

An emissions commitment drawn at a sub-sectoral boundary relocates emissions rather than reducing them wherever the system cannot build its way into compliance inside that boundary. The commitment’s reported quantity falls because the regulated quantity falls; the national quantity need not fall. The instrument is not ineffective in the sense of doing nothing. It is effective at the boundary at which it is measured, and the measurement does not correspond to the quantity the commitment is intended to govern.

Two things can prevent a system from building its way into compliance, and the results separate them. Where the commitment is set at the conditional stringency, the commitment binds hard enough to displace demand on its own: ⟦12.76⟧ per cent of emissions fall outside the boundary over the reporting window with no capital constraint in force at all, and imposing one changes that figure by about a tenth of a percentage point (§4.4.2). Where the commitment is set at the unconditional stringency, the commitment alone displaces almost nothing — ⟦0.14⟧ per cent — and a capital budget a few per cent short of the least-cost requirement raises it to between ⟦3.9⟧ and ⟦8.8⟧ per cent.

Two consequences follow for instrument design. First, the financing precondition is not an implementation detail to be sequenced after the target is set; at the less stringent of the two commitments it is the determinant of whether the target achieves national abatement at all, and a commitment-setting process that treats finance as a subsequent mobilisation problem has deferred the variable that decides the outcome. Second, the conditional and unconditional structure of a Nationally Determined Contribution is, read through this result, a structure of exactly the right kind: it conditions the stringency of the commitment on the availability of external capital. The result supports that architecture and locates two weaknesses. The conditionality attaches to the stringency of the target and not to the boundary at which compliance is measured; and at the conditional stringency the boundary leaks whether or not the external capital arrives.

The formulation cannot carry this argument further. Policy is exogenous throughout: the cap trajectory and the carbon price are constructed before the solve and are never fed back (§3.7.3). Every leakage result is a comparative-statics result across pre-specified cap paths, and not a statement about how policy would evolve in response to the leakage it discovers. A policymaker observing leakage at the measured scale would act; this model cannot say how.

### 5.4.3 Effect of tightening a grid-only cap

Tightening a grid-only cap does not raise national emissions over the reporting window. Comparing a cap declining after 2035 against one held flat, national emissions over 2025 to 2045 are lower under the declining cap, with and without a capital budget in force (§4.4.3). The increase previously reported arises only over the full solved horizon and only where a budget binds simultaneously, from emissions after 2045 that the reporting window excludes.

The withdrawal of the reversal does not restore the instrument. Two statements survive and both are consequential.

The abatement achieved nationally is a fraction of that achieved at the boundary. Under a binding capital budget the additional diesel generation induced by the tighter cap offsets approximately two thirds of a tonne for each grid tonne abated, and the leakage share rises as the cap tightens; with the cap alone the offset is approximately three tenths of a tonne per grid tonne (§4.4.3). A grid-only instrument is therefore an inefficient route to national abatement even where it does not reverse, and its efficiency is itself a function of the capital constraint rather than of the cap.

Part of the response precedes the tightening. Under the declining cap the additional self-generation appears between 2031 and 2034, while the declining cap is still identical to the flat one (§4.4.3). The behaviour is a property of perfect foresight, which permits anticipation of a future constraint (§3.7.2). Read as a modelling artefact it is a limitation. Read as a statement about announced policy it is a hypothesis the formulation cannot test: a credible announced tightening may induce substitution before it takes effect, and a model with a receding horizon would be required to establish whether that occurs and at what rate.

### 5.4.4 The cost–emissions frontier and the meaning of a dominated point

Scanning the cap from its loosest to its tightest feasible value traces a frontier that bends backward under public financing: beyond a threshold, tightening the cap raises total national emissions while raising system cost (§4.4.5). The bend appears at both public capital levels, and the service-financed arms do not bend over the horizon.

The bend is not a numerical curiosity. A point beyond the turning point is dominated: another point on the same frontier achieves lower national emissions at lower system cost. A planner adopting a cap stringency in that region has selected an instrument setting that is worse on both of the axes the instrument is meant to improve. The set of such settings is not small — at the tighter capital level the majority of sampled cap stringencies are dominated (§4.4.5).

The property responsible is structural rather than parametric. Because self-generation is unbounded, the frontier need have no infeasible end: load can always leave the grid rather than the problem becoming infeasible (§3.7.6). A cost–emissions frontier computed on the capped quantity alone shows no such behaviour, and every sampled point remains non-dominated on that axis. Reporting both axes is therefore not a presentational choice; the two axes support opposite conclusions about the same instrument, and a study reporting only the regulated quantity would conclude that tightening always helps.

The tight end of the frontier is not located, and no statement is made about where infeasibility begins (§4.4.5).

### 5.4.5 Carbon pricing under a binding capital budget

A carbon price applied where the public capital budget binds produces abatement, and the increase in system cost exceeds the carbon payment collected, so the instrument is not a pure transfer (§4.4.4). The quantity of abatement it achieves is nonetheless strongly conditioned by the budget: at the tier named tight a price of ⟦150⟧ dollars per tonne abates ⟦12.7⟧ per cent of cumulative emissions, against ⟦69.8⟧ per cent for the same price with the budget unconstrained.

The reading is the same as §4.4.2’s reached through a different instrument. A capital constraint does not prevent abatement; it determines which abatement options can be financed, and therefore how much of the response a price signal can call forth. A carbon price and a capital constraint are not independent instruments acting on the same system, and the effectiveness of the first is contingent on the second.

This result replaces a stronger claim held under the preceding specification, that a binding envelope nullified the price entirely (§4.1.5). The weaker claim is the defensible one and is reported as such.

## 5.5 Planning across demand realisations

### 5.5.1 Adequacy of a committed plan

A plan optimised against the central demand realisation and held fixed is infeasible against ⟦50⟧ of ⟦100⟧ sampled realisations; the same draws solved with the plan free are feasible in all ⟦100⟧ (§4.5.2).

The structure of the failures is sharper than the count. The feasible and infeasible draws do not overlap in demand growth: every draw below the central growth assumption is feasible and every draw above it is not, with the central assumption itself the boundary. The deterministically-optimal plan therefore carries no adequacy margin in the strict sense — not a small margin, but none, since the plan fails at the first realisation exceeding the assumption it was optimised against.

The result follows from cost-minimisation under a single assumed future, which gives the plan no reason to carry capacity that future does not require. It is a property of the planning method rather than of this model’s parameter values, and it applies to any deterministically-optimal capacity plan whose demand projection is a point estimate. A planning process adopting such a plan has implicitly assumed that demand will not exceed its own central projection.

For Nigerian practice the implication is a question about the status of the demand projection in a national plan. A projection used as a planning input is a central estimate with dispersion around it; a projection used as the single future against which capacity is optimised is, in effect, treated as a ceiling. The two uses require different institutional treatment, and the difference is not visible in the plan document.

### 5.5.2 Specification of the service standard

The reliability standard may be written as a budget over the whole horizon or as a constraint applying in each year. The two are not equivalent, and the horizon form is the weaker, permitting years above the annual standard provided the horizon total remains within budget (§4.5.3).

On the corrected model the difference is observable only at the measured availability arm, where the horizon form leaves two years above the ten per cent standard and expends approximately a fifth of the horizon budget while the annual form leaves none. At the nameplate arm neither form leaves any year above the standard (§4.5.3).

Two readings follow. The specification matters where the system is stressed and not where it is not, which is the circumstance in which a drafting choice is least likely to receive attention. And the difference is observable in this study only under the availability arm the thesis treats as the declared lower bound, so the magnitude of the drafting consequence is conditional on the same unmeasured parameter that dominates §5.2.3.

The comparison establishing which determinant each form understates cannot be reproduced: all eight cells resolve at the tightest grid value in both forms, and the determinant is recorded as indeterminate (§4.5.3). That the corrected model requires no coarser grid than the tightest sampled is itself a statement about the system — the configurations concerned achieve essentially perfect reliability — but it removes the resolution the comparison required.

### 5.5.3 The value of foresight and what the bound does not separate

The difference between the expected cost of a plan committed before the realisation is known and the expected cost of plans chosen with perfect foresight is approximately one third of the committed-plan expected cost (§4.5.4). The quantity bounds the value of the stochastic solution and the expected value of perfect information jointly and does not separate them (Birge and Louveaux 2011, ch. 4).

The non-separation is the material limitation and is not a technicality. The value of a stochastic formulation is the value of hedging against a known distribution; the value of perfect information is the value of knowing the realisation in advance, which no planner can purchase. A bound containing both establishes only that the two together are worth no more than the stated quantity. A planner cannot read it as the return on building a stochastic planning capability, and this study does not locate the value within the interval (§1.6.7).

Both expectations are means over a right-skewed distribution and are reported with their medians; the bound is driven substantially by the upper tail of the committed-plan distribution, which is the tail reported as infeasibility in §4.5.2 (§4.5.4). The right tail is amplified by compounding a growth-rate perturbation across a thirty-year horizon, and the dispersion parameter generating it is a model-internal calibration with no external source (§3.7.5). The bound’s magnitude is therefore conditioned by an unsourced quantity, while the existence of a substantial gap between the two expectations is not.

Both legs run with the public capital budget unconstrained, so the bound is the value of foresight to a planner not simultaneously capital constrained (§4.5.4). Given §5.4.1, foresight to a capital-constrained planner is a different and unmeasured quantity.

### 5.5.4 The adequacy result against reserve-margin practice

The adequacy results above are produced under a firm-capacity constraint whose reserve requirement is fixed in megawatts while peak demand grows, so the implied fractional margin falls from ⟦19.5⟧ to ⟦6.4⟧ per cent across the horizon (§3.7.4). The requirement is also an operating quantity — a reserve sized against the largest single contingency — used as a planning standard, which is a different class of quantity.

The comparison this invite is against the planning reserve margin Nigerian practice specifies, and it is not made here: no primary statement of that requirement was obtained.

What can be stated without that source is the direction. A margin declining to single figures by the end of the horizon is below the range in which planning reserve margins are ordinarily set, and the adequacy results reported in §4.5 are accordingly optimistic by a margin this study does not quantify. The failure rate of §5.5.1 is therefore a floor on the problem rather than an estimate of it.

## 5.6 The results against the reviewed corpus

The comparison below is bounded to the corpus of optimisation-based power-system planning studies for sub-Saharan Africa identified by the systematic review reported in Chapter 2. No claim is made concerning the wider capacity-expansion literature, which the review did not search, and the review’s own limitations — a retrieval failure of approximately one quarter of reports sought at full text, a pre-committed screening stop, and single-reviewer screening with two registered validation steps not executed — bound every statement of absence below (§2.6).

The comparison is structural rather than numerical. The review extracted dimensional coverage and the treatment of financing; it did not extract system costs, capacity mixes or sensitivity magnitudes, so the corpus does not support a comparison of this study’s figures against published figures.

**Financing structure.** No study in the corpus determines financing structure or the cost of capital within its model, and 63.1 per cent of those supplying a value give no source or argument for it (§2.5.1, §2.5.2, §2.7.1). The present study adds a quantity the corpus cannot supply: where a concessional facility of finite size exists, deciding how to allocate it across vintages is worth the majority of what the facility is worth at all, and that decision is foreclosed by any formulation carrying the cost of capital as a scalar (§5.3.1). The omission is therefore not a documentation failure but a structural one, and it bites hardest where concessional finance is the binding resource — which is where sub-Saharan African planning problems are ordinarily posed.

**The one direct tension.** One study in the corpus varies the cost of capital from 5 to 15 per cent — a range wider than the full spread of point values reported elsewhere in the corpus — and reports that the least-cost technology mix reorganises across it (§2.5.2). The present study finds that financing parameters move system cost by approximately ⟦11.5⟧ per cent while the terminal composition of the portfolio moves by at most ⟦0.47⟧ per cent in solar and ⟦0.32⟧ per cent in storage, with the timing of investment responding substantially more than the composition (§4.2.4). The two results are not reconcilable from the information the review extracted, and three explanations are available: the ranges differ in width, the systems differ in the substitutability of their technology options, or the quantity reported as reorganisation in that study is a timing effect of the kind this study separates. The comparison is reported as unresolved.

**Availability and deliverability.** The review defines availability and deliverability as distinct dimensions and records that in gas-dependent systems deliverability is predominantly represented as a fuel-supply constraint, so a model representing availability alone will report an adequate system where a deliverability constraint would report a shortfall (§2.3.2). This study’s Question 1 result is an instance of that distinction operating at the level of cost rather than adequacy: the quantity of installed capacity is not what determines the cost of the pathway, and the two largest gas-side families concern whether existing plant can run and whether fuel reaches it (§5.2.1). The corpus’s conceptual distinction is supported, and extended from a statement about what a model can detect to a statement about what dominates its output.

**The boundary of an emissions constraint.** The review found that the relationship between a constraint drawn at a sub-sectoral boundary and the national quantity it is intended to govern is not established by the reviewed studies (§2.7.1). The present study measures that relationship and reports that it is mediated by a quantity the reviewed studies do not represent: the availability of capital to finance compliance inside the boundary (§5.4.1). The gap is therefore not only unaddressed in the corpus but unaddressable within the corpus’s dominant treatment of financing, since a model supplying the cost of capital as a scalar and imposing no volume constraint on it cannot represent a system unable to finance compliance.

**Access.** Gap 2 of §2.7.1, the partition of access and acceptability by model class, is inherited by this study rather than narrowed by it. The model is single-node, contains no connection decision, and represents self-generation as a failure of delivery to connected demand rather than as a representation of access (§1.6.2, §2.7.2, §3.7.3). The leakage results of §5.4 concern load already connected to the grid, and nothing in this chapter should be read as a statement about unconnected populations.

## 5.7 Implications for policy and practice

The implications below follow from the results discussed above and are bounded by §1.6.7. Each names the result it rests on. None extends to the siting of capacity, the selection of populations for connection, an optimal financing structure, or a carbon price at which diesel generation is displaced, none of which the formulation identifies.

**Measurement before refinement.** A fuel-agnostic availability factor for the existing gas fleet, published at a resolution separating mechanical and atmospheric availability from feedstock unavailability, is worth more to the quality of a national planning exercise than the refinement of every other cost input in this model combined (§5.2.3). The quantity is held by the system operator and the regulator rather than requiring new measurement, and the action it implies is a disclosure and classification exercise.

**A commitment relocates emissions where the system cannot build its way into compliance.** At the unconditional stringency, a capital shortfall of the order of a few per cent below the least-cost requirement raises out-of-boundary emissions from ⟦0.14⟧ to between ⟦3.9⟧ and ⟦8.8⟧ per cent of the national total; at the conditional stringency the commitment displaces ⟦12.76⟧ per cent on its own, and the capital constraint adds little (§5.4.1, §5.4.2). The sequencing of concessional and climate finance against the adoption of a commitment is therefore a determinant of the commitment’s national effect at the less stringent target, and at the more stringent one the boundary itself is the binding problem.

**A private-capital channel is a budgetary instrument, not an abatement instrument.** Its value is the public capital it relieves, it is worth approximately nothing where no public capital constraint binds, and at the current commitment its entire measured value equals the cost of the public envelope it displaces (§5.3.2). It should be appraised against the alternative of expanding that envelope, and not credited with abatement.

**Concessional finance at current scale changes who pays, not what is built.** At approximately one tenth of the capital programme the envelope is inframarginal: it is exhausted at every build decision, the marginal dollar is commercial, and the terminal plan is unchanged across the full range of envelope scales examined (§5.3.3). A facility intended to change the composition of a national plan would have to be larger than this by an order of magnitude, and an eligibility rule restricting it to solar and storage capital confines it to approximately half of the capital programme (§5.2.2).

**Grid-boundary instruments should be evaluated on the national quantity.** The cost–emissions frontier is non-dominated at every sampled point when read on the regulated quantity and substantially dominated when read on the national quantity (§5.4.4). An instrument appraisal conducted on the regulated quantity alone will not detect the settings at which tightening is worse on both axes.

**A deterministic least-cost plan is not a plan with an adequacy margin.** Such a plan fails at the first realisation exceeding the projection it was optimised against (§5.5.1). Where a national plan is produced by deterministic least-cost optimisation, the adequacy margin has to be imposed as an explicit constraint rather than expected to emerge from the optimisation, and the reserve requirement imposed should be a planning reserve margin rather than an operating reserve (§5.5.4).

## 5.8 Limitations

The limitations below condition the interpretations above. They are drawn from §3.7 and from the limitations register reproduced as Appendix B, and are organised on the three classes §3.7.1 establishes. The direction of bias is stated where it is known and the absence of a signed direction is stated where it is not.

### 5.8.1 Method boundaries

**No stochastic programming with recourse.** The optimiser never hedges against a distribution within a single programme. The value of the formulation not solved is bounded above but not located, and the bound contains both the value of the stochastic solution and the expected value of perfect information (§3.7.2, §5.5.3).

**No chance constraints and no integer decisions.** Reliability is imposed as a deterministic bound on shortfall rather than as a probabilistic standard. The formulation is kept linear so that duals remain available, and every shadow price discussed above depends on that choice (§3.7.2).

**A single solver at one tolerance.** Where the optimal face is flat the vertex returned is solver-dependent. The class of result exposed to this is identified in §3.6.10, and it includes the division between publicly and service-financed solar, which is reported as unidentified for that reason (§5.3.2).

### 5.8.2 Structural simplifications

**Perfect foresight.** Every year’s decision sees every other year’s data. This understates the cost of uncertainty and overstates how efficiently a planner could sequence investment. It is the direct cause of the anticipatory self-generation discussed in §5.4.3, and it bounds what §5.5 can establish: the adequacy failure is measured by fixing a foresighted plan and testing it, which is a probe of the gap rather than a closure of it (§3.7.2, §3.7.3).

**Single-node spatial resolution.** Nigeria is one node, with no locational variable, no transmission constraint and no zonal balance. A plan optimal nationally may require network reinforcement the model neither prices nor constrains against. The direction cannot be signed without knowing whether Nigerian network bottlenecks bear more heavily on distributed solar or on concentrated gas and hydro (§3.7.3).

**No integer structure.** No unit commitment, no minimum stable generation, no ramping constraint, no start-up cost and no lumpy build. Three of the four bias in the same direction, understating the cost and emissions of operating thermal plant flexibly and overstating how freely gas can follow a residual-demand shape net of solar; continuous capacity addition biases total system cost downward (§3.7.3). The gas-side results of §5.2 are therefore conservative with respect to the difficulty of operating the existing fleet.

**Storage as an energy balance rather than a state of charge.** The seasonal constraint imposes no ordering between periods within a season, so a reported dispatch is not guaranteed chronologically feasible. The representation is more permissive than a chronological one and therefore favours storage (§3.7.3). Storage magnitude is in any case not a cost optimum but a residual of the firm-capacity constraint and the capacity credit applied to it (§4.2.3), so storage quantities are not interpreted in this chapter as an investment recommendation.

**Demand is exogenous and price-inelastic.** A high-tariff scenario that would in reality suppress some load appears entirely as a capacity and cost consequence, overstating the capacity required under such a regime. That is the regime the service-financed channel implies, so the bias falls where the central contribution is measured (§3.7.3). The conclusions of §5.3.2 concerning the channel’s budgetary value are unaffected, since they rest on the capital constraint rather than on the demand response; the conclusions concerning capacity under such a regime are not.

**System boundary and network losses.** Cross-border trade is absent. The busbar factor recognising that a generator at the point of use displaces more than one unit of busbar generation defaults to unity, so transmission losses are not credited to self-generation. That default understates how much self-generation displaces, which is a conservative bias against the leakage results of §5.4 rather than one inflating them (§3.7.3).

**Policy is exogenous.** No feedback from the plan to the policy exists, and no rule of the form “if leakage exceeds a threshold, tighten further” is represented. Every result in §5.4 is comparative statics across pre-specified cap paths (§3.7.3, §5.4.2).

**Hydro carries no operating cost and its trajectory is exogenous.** The benefit attributed to hydro expansion is accordingly an upper bound, and hydro is reported as a bound rather than as a lever for that reason (§3.7.3, §4.2.5). The solar-dominant character of the central pathway is contingent on hydro not expanding on the trajectory the national commitment implies, and the cost difference between the two hydro trajectories exceeds every ranked family (§4.2.5).

**Technology learning is exogenous.** No expression ties a cost in any year to anything the model has built, so neither the cost declines a large domestic programme might induce nor the supply-chain pressure it might create is represented. Recorded as an omission rather than a signed bias (§3.7.3).

### 5.8.3 Provenance gaps

**Gas fleet availability.** No sourced central value exists; the arm offers a declared lower bound and an assumed upper bound, and the central configuration sits on the upper bound (§3.7.5). The consequence for interpretation is stated twice above: it is the dominant parameter (§5.2.3) and the bracket’s exposure is bounded, since results turning on feasibility or frontier position are invariant to the choice of arm while results turning on duals are not (§4.1.4).

**Position of the central configuration in the parameter distribution.** Across the parameter-uncertainty draws the central configuration’s system cost falls below the fifth percentile of the sampled distribution (§4.2.7). The central configuration is therefore an optimistic corner of the parameter space rather than a central tendency of it, and every cost figure reported from it should be read accordingly.

**Precision of the variance decomposition.** The first-order index assigned to gas availability carries a bootstrap confidence interval spanning a substantial range, so the “nine-tenths” statement is a point estimate with material sampling uncertainty attached, not a tightly estimated quantity (§4.2.7). The qualitative conclusion — that one parameter dominates and the remainder are small beside it — is robust to that interval; the specific fraction is not.

**Value of lost load.** Read from a data file carrying no source column and no classification, flat across every year. It prices unserved energy in every solve and is the backstop against which every reliability result is measured (§3.7.5).

**Capital cost bases are not Nigerian.** Solar and storage unit capital costs are United States series with no local adjustment, and the two available benchmark comparisons run in opposite directions (§3.7.5).

**Demand dispersion in the realisation treatment.** The standard deviation applied to the demand growth rate is a model-internal calibration with no external source, and a separate parameter characterises the same uncertain quantity differently for the variance-decomposition treatment; the two are unreconciled (§3.7.5, §5.5.3).

**Self-generation cost and emission factors.** The three arms are three market segments rather than an ordered sensitivity on one quantity, and the model applies one segment to the entire self-generation volume where the actual mix spans all three. The leakage magnitudes of §5.4 are reported on the commercial and industrial diesel case (§3.7.5).

**Composition of the firm-capacity constraint.** Three of the four weakly identified inputs to that constraint render adequacy easier to satisfy in the model than in the system it represents, and because they enter the same constraint they compound rather than offset (§3.7.4). The adequacy results of §5.5 are optimistic by an unquantified margin.

### 5.8.4 Results bounded by their own grids

Where a threshold is detected across a bounded grid and resolves at a grid boundary, the reported magnitude is a lower bound rather than a confirmed measurement (§3.7.7). Two instances bear directly on this chapter. All eight reliability cells resolve at the tightest grid value in both forms of the service standard, which is what prevents the comparison of §5.5.2 from being completed. The tight end of the cost–emissions frontier is unlocated in all four arms, so §5.4.4 makes no statement about where infeasibility begins.

### 5.8.5 Two statements withheld

Two quantities a reader might expect are not reported, and the reasons differ. The capital-sufficiency verdict printed by the capital sweep keys on unserved energy, which is fixed at zero by construction, so the budget constraint’s dual is the reportable quantity and the printed verdict is not used anywhere in this thesis. And one finding of the frozen record could not be verified against the external sources it names, which were never read in the primary; it is recorded as unverifiable and nothing in this chapter rests on it.

## 5.9 Further work

The items below are ordered by the ratio of what they would resolve to what they would cost. Each names the condition it would discharge.

**A fuel-agnostic availability factor for the existing gas fleet.** The single highest-value item, for the reasons given in §5.2.3 and §5.7. It would narrow the model’s dominant prior, discharge the double duty the capacity credit currently performs, and permit the adequacy results of §5.5 to be read without the optimism condition of §3.7.4. It requires disclosure and classification of records already held rather than new measurement.

**A planning reserve margin sourced as such.** The present requirement is an operating quantity used as a planning standard and is fixed in megawatts against a growing peak (§3.7.4, §5.5.4). Sourcing a planning reserve margin, or establishing that Nigerian practice specifies none, would place the adequacy results on a standard rather than on an analyst construction.

**A hydro operating-cost term.** Hydro presently carries neither a capital nor an operating cost term, which is why its trajectory is reported as a bound rather than ranked as a lever (§4.2.5, §5.8.2). The change is specified and a tested regression arm reproducing current behaviour exists, so the work is implementation and re-derivation rather than design.

**The self-generation busbar factor.** The factor defaults to unity, so transmission losses are not credited to self-generation and the leakage results are conservative by approximately the loss factor (§3.7.3, §5.8.2). The change is specified with a regression arm built, and it would remove a known conservative bias from the chapter’s principal policy result.

**Storage state of charge.** A chronological storage representation would replace the seasonal energy balance, whose permissiveness favours storage (§3.7.3). The change is deferred and unbuilt, and it is the largest of the structural items.

**Separating the value of the stochastic solution from the value of perfect information.** Solving the two-stage recourse problem, rather than bounding it from two evaluations, would locate the quantity within the interval §5.5.3 reports and would convert a bound into a decision input for a planning institution (§1.6.4, §3.6.11).

**The leakage counterfactual.** An economy-wide cap, in which self-generation falls inside the regulated quantity, would make leakage structurally impossible and would quantify the cost of the boundary directly rather than by comparison across cap paths. The configuration is not exposed by any runner at present.

**Extension of the comparison to the wider literature.** The comparison in §5.6 is bounded to the sub-Saharan African corpus the review searched, and the one direct tension it identifies is unresolved from the information the review extracted (§5.6). A targeted extraction over that corpus, or a declared supplementary search beyond it, would establish whether the reorganisation of technology mix reported in that study concerns terminal composition or build timing.

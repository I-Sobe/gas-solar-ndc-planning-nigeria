# Chapter 1 — Introduction

## 1.1 The Nigerian power system 

### 1.1.1 Grid supply and its boundary

Nigeria’s grid delivered 37,093.70 GWh in 2024 (NERC, 2024, Annual reports, p 145). That quantity is the base from which every projection in this thesis scales, and it excludes electricity generated by households and firms on their own plant. The excluded quantity is large. Backup generation across sub-Saharan Africa amounted to approximately 40 TWh from 40 GW of installed plant in 2018, of which Nigeria accounted for almost half, some 18 TWh from roughly 9 GW (International Energy Agency \[IEA\], 2019, p. 63). The model developed in this thesis accordingly represents approximately two-thirds of national electricity consumption, and its conclusions concern the grid’s service obligation rather than national demand (§3.2.4).

### 1.1.2 Gas deliverability

The gas fleet’s operating limit in most years is the quantity of fuel that reaches it rather than the nameplate capacity it holds. Annual deliverability is anchored at 89.27 TWh of thermal energy in 2025. The anchor rests on one measured quantity, the regulator’s 2024 generation data, together with an assumed fleet efficiency and an assumed heating value; the implied gas volume is that same figure restated, not an independent source (§3.3.2).

The distance between that figure and stated policy defines the constraint this thesis represents. The Gas Master Plan’s allocation of one billion standard cubic feet per day to the power sector is a statement of demand, whereas the anchor above is a measurement of delivery, and the two differ by approximately twenty-four per cent. A model adopting the plan’s level as its starting point would assume away the constraint under study. The planning chain is therefore used for the shape of the deliverability trajectory and not for its level (§3.3.2).

### 1.1.3 Open-cycle capacity and conversion

Sixteen open-cycle plants in the installed fleet are convertible to combined cycle. Conversion raises output per unit of a constrained fuel without requiring additional gas, and eligibility for it is determined by observed operating condition rather than assumed: each plant’s mean performance availability factor across the regulator’s 2024, 2025/Q4 and 2026/Q1 tables is banded into three tiers, and tier membership follows from that measurement (§3.3.3). Conversion is accordingly a fuel-efficiency instrument rather than a capacity instrument, and is treated in this thesis as part of the gas supply problem.

### 1.1.4 Self-generation

Demand the grid does not meet is met by privately owned diesel and petrol plant. Self-generation enters the model as a priced backstop, unbounded in the absence of a policy constraint, since constraining it would make infeasibility rather than cost the signal of an inadequate plan (§3.3.7). It is represented as three market segments: large commercial and industrial diesel at short-run marginal cost, part-loaded commercial and industrial diesel at full levelised cost, and small petrol plant, carrying emission factors of 0.729, 0.902 and 4.638 tonnes of carbon dioxide per megawatt-hour respectively, of which the second is the central case (§3.7.4). The segments differ by a factor exceeding six.

### 1.1.5 The constraint chain

The three constraints above are not independent. Gas that is not delivered is output the fleet cannot produce; output the fleet cannot produce is demand the grid does not serve; and demand the grid does not serve is met at an emission factor several times that of grid supply. The chain runs from a midstream supply constraint to a national emissions outcome, and it crosses a commitment boundary drawn around the grid.

## 1.2 The commitment, the planning problem and the statutory framework

### 1.2.1 The decarbonisation commitment and its boundary

Nigeria’s Nationally Determined Contribution states an emissions reduction target for the energy sector, of which gas-fired electricity generation is one component. It states no power-sector share of that target, so an allocation rule is unavoidable, and the rule adopted assigns abatement to power in proportion to its share of sector emissions (§3.5.2). The commitment’s gas capacity figure is derated rather than nameplate: the stated 17 GW corresponds to a nameplate of 22,113 MW at 2035 at the derating factor the series implies, and that nameplate is 2.02 times the 10,947 MW starting fleet, reproducing the commitment’s own description of an approximate doubling where the derated figure does not.

The boundary of that commitment conditions every policy result in this thesis. A target expressed for grid-supplied generation does not reach electricity that households and firms generate for themselves, and Chapter 2 establishes that the reviewed literature does not examine the relationship between a constraint drawn at such a boundary and the national quantity it governs (§2.7.1). The model applies the cap to the grid-supplied quantity, consistent with the sub-sectoral basis of the commitment, and leaves self-generation outside it (§3.5.3).

### 1.2.2 The planning problem

The question posed to a capacity-expansion model in this setting is narrower than it first appears. It is not what Nigeria should build in order to electrify, which requires representations of connection and of unconnected demand that this formulation does not contain (§2.7.2). It is what the grid must build to meet the demand placed upon it under a fuel-supply constraint, what that costs, how the cost is financed, and what emissions the resulting plan produces against a commitment made nationally.

### 1.2.3 The statutory framework

The regulatory framework governing Nigerian electricity changed in 2023, and the change was one of legislative competence rather than of physical system.

Electricity is an item of the Concurrent Legislative List, Part II of the Second Schedule to the Constitution, on which both the Federation and the States may legislate. What altered was the extent of State competence. The Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999 (Fifth Alteration) (No. 17) Act, 2023, section 2, altered paragraph 14(b) of that Part by deleting, after the word “areas”, the words “not covered by a national grid system”, with effect from 3 March 2023. State legislative power over generation, transmission and distribution had previously been confined to areas outside the national grid system; the alteration removed that confinement. The Electricity Act, 2023 then repealed the Electric Power Sector Reform Act, No. 6 of 2005 and re-enacted the framework for the Nigerian electricity supply industry in 233 sections across 22 Parts, including provision for a National Integrated Electricity Policy and Strategic Implementation Plan (ss. 3–5), an independent system operator (ss. 15–32), promotion of generation from renewable energy (s. 80), partnerships to facilitate transmission within a State (s. 112), and renewable energy and energy efficiency (ss. 164–171)

Regulation, licensing and retail tariff-setting within a State’s borders devolve under this framework. The synchronised transmission system, system operation, interstate transactions and wholesale settlement do not, and a State purchasing bulk supply from the national grid remains exposed to the upstream generation costs that supply carries. The Electricity Act further requires a national integrated policy and implementation plan, so national resource planning is a statutory obligation under the new framework.

Two consequences follow for the present study. The framework described above was in force before the model’s first solve year: the alteration commenced in March 2023 and the Electricity Act was assented in June 2023, while the model’s base year is 2024 and its horizon opens in 2025. The statutory framework is therefore the one in force across the whole modelled period. What the model does not represent is the progressive transfer of regulatory oversight that has proceeded since, State by State.

The unit of analysis also survives the change. The quantity computed is the cost of bulk supply under a fuel-supply constraint and a capital constraint, together with the emissions that supply produces, and that problem remains national under the framework above. The formulation does not address who builds, under whose licence, or how costs are allocated between federal and State balance sheets. Three of its parameters are represented as single national values where legislative competence is now divided, and those are identified in §1.6.6.

## 1.3 The capital requirement

The pathway described above is capital-intensive in a measurable sense. In the central solution the discounted capital charge accounts for 40.0 per cent of system cost, fuel for 32.2 per cent and operation and maintenance for 27.8 per cent. Within the capital charge, public solar is the largest line at 13.1 per cent of system cost, followed by new gas at 10.2 per cent and conversion of existing open-cycle plant at 10.1 per cent (§3.4.2).

The capital charge is an annuity on capital raised and not the capital itself; gross capital expenditure over the full horizon, in present value, is ⟦43.5⟧ per cent of system cost. The rate at which capital expenditure is annualised therefore determines the standing of capital-intensive technologies against fuel-intensive ones in a least-cost solution.

The reviewed literature supplies that rate as an input. Chapter 2 reports that in sixty-five of seventy-five studies of optimisation-based power-system planning in sub-Saharan Africa the cost of capital is a supplied value, that in ten no financing parameter is stated, and that in none is financing structure or the cost of capital determined within the model. Of the sixty-five, 63.1 per cent apply a value for which no source or argument is given (§2.5.1, §2.5.2).

Two properties of Nigerian conditions bear on that treatment. Capital for energy infrastructure in developing economies differs materially by country, by technology and by delivery mode, and the difference is large enough to reorder a least-cost ranking (Ondraczek et al., 2015; Egli et al., 2019; Steffen, 2020). The below-market capital actually available is also finite. Concessional finance is cheaper than commercial finance at every configuration examined in this thesis, so a formulation that does not bound its quantity funds the entire programme concessionally and the question of capital structure does not arise (§3.4.3). The constraint that renders the structure a decision is therefore a volume limit, and the magnitude of that limit relative to the programme’s capital requirement determines whether a financing instrument changes a plan or subsidises one. The formulation’s response, the envelope’s magnitude and the basis on which that magnitude is set are given in §3.4.3.

## 1.4 Research questions

Four questions follow from the gaps identified in Chapter 2 and from the capabilities and boundaries of the formulation specified in Chapter 3. They proceed from the physical constraints on the expansion pathway to the financing and policy instruments acting upon it. Formulations not answerable by the model are given in §1.6.7.

**Question 1.** *What limits the least-cost expansion pathway for Nigeria’s grid to 2045, and what is the system-cost value of relieving each limit?*

The limits examined are the gas fleet’s availability, the deliverability of gas to that fleet, the conversion of open-cycle to combined-cycle plant, and the retirement schedule. Each is measured as the span of system cost across a declared arm family, and the question is answered by a ranked table reporting the span of every swept family, of which those limits are four. The span of the capital-recovery parameter is reported among them, locating the magnitude of the financing parameter against the physical ones; that comparison concerns the span of a supplied parameter and is distinct from Question 2, which concerns the consequences of determining capital structure within the model.

A family enters the ranked table only where each of its arms describes an alternative state the system could occupy. Arms that exist to decompose the objective rather than to describe such a state are reported outside the table with their spans given, since their magnitudes answer a different question. Hydro expansion is likewise reported as a bound rather than as a lever: the hydro trajectory is exogenous, is subject to no build-rate constraint, and carries no cost term of any kind in the objective, neither capital nor operating (Appendix B, L95), so its span measures the value of a costless input rather than the cost of a constraint. Each limit is measured without a policy constraint and under a binding emissions commitment, since the value of relieving a physical limit is not invariant to whether such a commitment is in force.

**Question 2.** *When the division of each vintage’s capital between concessional and commercial sources is determined within the model rather than supplied to it, what does that determination change, and under what conditions does it change nothing?*

The question concerns system cost, total installed capacity and the dispersion of cost across realisations. It is not asked of the division between publicly and privately financed solar, which lies on a flat optimal face and is not identified by the formulation (§3.4.4), and it is asked within a concessional envelope restricted to renewable capital, a restriction that is a declared modelling assumption rather than a rule of the facility from which the envelope’s scale is taken (§3.4.3).

**Question 3.** *What determines the quantity of emissions falling outside the grid commitment boundary, and how does tightening a constraint drawn at that boundary affect national emissions?*

The determinants examined are the stringency of the emissions constraint, the scarcity of public capital, the rate at which grid revenue is collected, and the financing channel through which capacity is raised. Policy enters as an exogenous constraint and the formulation contains no feedback from the plan to the policy. The constraint examined is the grid’s service obligation and the deliverability of supply against it, and not the extension of access to unconnected demand, which the formulation does not represent (§2.7.2, §3.7.3).

**Question 4.** *Does a plan optimised against a single deterministic future retain capacity adequacy across demand realisations around that future; does the specification of the service standard change what is observed; and what upper bound may be placed on the value of a formulation that does not assume a single future?*

Capacity adequacy denotes the firm-capacity condition of §3.1.7. It is distinct from the revenue-adequacy condition governing privately financed capacity (§3.4.5) and from the capital-case tier bearing the same name, which §3.4.4 identifies as a grid label rather than an economic characterisation. The margin reported is conditioned by §3.7.4: three of the four weakly identified inputs to the firm-capacity constraint render adequacy easier to satisfy in the model than in the system it represents, so the measured margin is an optimistic one. The question is answered by fixed-plan re-dispatch across sampled realisations, by comparison of two forms of the service standard, and by a bound derived from two evaluations. It is not answered by stochastic programming with recourse, which this study does not perform (§3.6.11). It responds to the energy-security dimensional framework of §2.3.2 rather than to a numbered gap in §2.7.1.

### 1.4.1 Relation of the questions to the identified gaps

Question 1 addresses the physical component of the planning problem and supplies the ordering within which the financing contribution is located. Question 2 addresses Gap 1 of §2.7.1, the supply of the cost of capital as an undefended input. Question 3 addresses Gap 3, the unexamined relationship between a constraint’s boundary and the national quantity it governs. Gap 2, the partition of access and acceptability by model class, is inherited by this study rather than narrowed by it, for the reasons given in §2.7.2.

## 1.5 Contribution

### 1.5.1 The formulation

The contribution of this thesis is a capacity-expansion formulation in which the division of each vintage’s capital between a concessional tranche and a commercial tranche is a decision variable, bounded by a finite concessional envelope and disciplined by a coverage-ratio condition on privately financed capacity. Each tranche is annualised at its own rate over its own tenor, and both capital recovery factors and their discount weights are precomputed constants, so the financing decision is a volume split at fixed prices and the programme remains linear (§3.4.2). The formulation is applied to the problem of maintaining capacity adequacy in a fuel-constrained system under a sub-sectoral emissions commitment.

### 1.5.2 Scope of the novelty claim

Within the corpus of optimisation-based power-system planning studies for sub-Saharan Africa identified by the systematic review reported in Chapter 2, no study determines financing structure or the realised cost of capital within the model. The formulation presented in §3.4.2 does so. No claim is made concerning the wider capacity-expansion literature, which the review did not search, and §2.6 states that boundary in the terms the review’s protocol requires.

Two conditions attach to the claim. The restriction of concessional eligibility to renewable capital is a declared modelling assumption and not a rule of the facility from which the envelope’s scale is taken: no technology eligibility rule appears in that facility’s governing document, and no general convention excluding fossil generation from multilateral concessional finance was established for the relevant institution. The consequence is directional and is stated in §3.4.3, being that the envelope is a renewable-capital instrument by construction, so the model cannot identify a concessional dollar better deployed on gas capacity. The mechanism the service-financed channel represents is also generic private capital mobilisation, comprising budget decoupling together with a revenue-adequacy test; nothing in the formulation is specific to a service contract as distinct from any other structure moving capital off the public balance sheet and subjecting it to such a test (§3.4.6).

### 1.5.3 Conditionality of the financing channel’s value

The value of the service-financed channel is conditional on the public capital budget binding, and the conditionality is itself the principal result of the financing analysis. At the central demand case under no policy the budget does not bind, and the measured difference between the two financing regimes is indistinguishable from zero.

The evidence for the conditionality is a separation rather than a point estimate. Across thirty-six configurations, in the eighteen cells where the public capital budget’s shadow price is zero the channel’s value is at most −0.61 United States dollars, which is the agreement between two solutions of the same plan. In the eighteen cells where that shadow price is positive it never falls below 653,600,175 dollars. One constraint binding or not binding separates the two outcomes exactly, and the channel’s value is monotone in that shadow price without inversion.

Where the constraint binds, the magnitude is substantial. Both financing regimes reach an identical optimum, being the same system cost, the same total solar capacity and the same storage, and the service-financed regime reaches it on 60.0 to 62.8 per cent less public capital. That comparison rests on the public capital budget constraint and on measured realised spend. The decomposition of the concessional advantage between rate and tenor, at 50.1 per cent to the tenor, is closed-form arithmetic on two capital recovery factors with no result file behind it, and is reported in §3.4.2 as such rather than as a measured outcome.

## 1.6 Scope and interpretation

### 1.6.1 Accounting basis of the objective

The quantity minimised is the discounted financial cost borne by the Nigerian power system, and not the social resource cost of the capacity it builds. Concessional finance does not reduce the cost of constructing a solar plant; it transfers part of that cost to the concessional lender, and the formulation prices the transfer rather than the resource (§3.4.1). Costs reported as savings are therefore savings to Nigeria, a part of which are transfers rather than reductions in resources consumed. The same accounting basis governs the public subsidy to privately financed capacity, which is a transfer inside the system boundary and is excluded from the objective.

### 1.6.2 Demand boundary

The model represents grid-supplied electricity, approximately two-thirds of national consumption (§1.1.1). Self-generation enters only as the priced backstop meeting grid demand the modelled system cannot (§3.3.7), and the absorption of existing self-generation onto the grid lies outside the formulation. The model contains no locational variable, no transmission-capacity constraint and no connection decision. It represents a failure of delivery to connected demand, which is a deliverability phenomenon, and not the extension of access (§2.7.2, §3.7.3).

### 1.6.3 Reporting window

The programme is solved over 2025 to 2054. Annual series, comprising capacity, emissions, shortfall and the cap trajectories, are reported to 2045, and the final nine years constitute an end-effect buffer. The objective is a full-horizon discounted quantity over the whole solved period, since truncating it at 2045 would require a terminal salvage treatment that the per-vintage formulation is constructed to avoid (§3.4.2). Terminal storage capacity at 2054 serves as a reproducibility signature and is not a result; the corresponding 2045 figure is 24,698 megawatt-hours.

### 1.6.4 Model class

Every solve is a deterministic, single-objective linear programme with fixed coefficients and perfect foresight. Bi-objective trade-offs are recovered by the ε-constraint method over two objective pairs, with non-dominance verified at each point. Uncertainty is treated at three levels, being scenario, parameter and realisation, which answer different questions and do not substitute for one another. The value of the recourse formulation this study does not solve is bounded above, from the two evaluations performed, for a first stage comprising solar and storage only; that bound contains both the value of the stochastic solution and the expected value of perfect information, and the two cannot be separated within it. There is no weighted-sum objective and no stochastic programming with recourse (§3.6.11).

### 1.6.5 Capital-case nomenclature

The capital-case tiers are grid labels. In units of the measured unconstrained requirement, the tier named `moderate` lies one-tenth of one per cent above that requirement and does not constrain the unconstrained plan, and the tier named `adequacy` is not fully funded under an emissions cap. Where a result turns on whether the budget binds, the constraint’s dual is the quantity to be read (§3.4.4).

### 1.6.6 Nationally represented parameters

Three parameters of the formulation are national scalars over a system in which the corresponding legislative competence is divided between the Federation and the States (§1.2.3): the service tariff, the rate at which grid revenue is collected, and the grid-service obligation. Each is a policy quantity a State regulator may set within its own borders. Results turning on any of the three describe a national average rather than the circumstances of a particular State.

### 1.6.7 Boundaries of inference

The following lie outside the formulation’s scope. The optimal financing structure, and the quantity of solar that should be service-financed: the division between the two channels lies on a flat optimal face and is not identified (§3.4.4). The siting of capacity and the selection of populations for connection: the model is single-node and contains no connection decision. The value of the stochastic solution: only an upper bound containing it is computed. The carbon price at which diesel generation is displaced: the swept range does not reach such a price, and the result establishes that rather than locating one (§3.5.5). The appropriate policy response to the displacement measured: policy is exogenous and no feedback from the plan to it exists. The probability that a plan fails: the formulation prices unserved energy and self-generation rather than constraining them, so feasibility probability is not a meaningful output.

### 1.6.8 Verification

The formulation was verified by an executable suite of 88 assertions of two kinds. The greater part are derived from properties the formulation must satisfy mathematically, independently of any claim made about it: accounting identities, coefficient constructions, and the agreement of each reported aggregate with an independent recomputation. A smaller number test consistency between the model as built and statements made about it in this thesis.

Of the 88 assertions, 81 pass, 7 fail and 0 are undeterminable (stage A 50/5/0, stage B 16/2/0, stage C 15/0/0). Each of the seven failures is recorded in the limitations register reproduced as Appendices B and C, six in Appendix B and one in Appendix C. Two of the seven fail by decision rather than by defect: they test whether annual build timing is invariant to the position of the horizon end, which the model does not exhibit and which §3.1.1 does not claim, while a companion assertion confirms the invariance of the quantities reported as results, which it does. None is silently carried.

Every reported result was produced with a single solver at one tolerance setting, and five of the passing assertions are conditional on that solver, so where the optimal face is flat the returned vertex is solver-dependent and §3.6.10 identifies the class of result exposed to it. The suite’s pass count is not a count of limitations resolved: two live register entries are characterised by assertions that pass, those assertions describing the structure as built rather than testing for the defect the entry records.

## 1.7 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reports a systematic review of optimisation-based power-system planning studies for sub-Saharan Africa, conducted to a registered protocol, and establishes the three gaps to which this study responds. Chapter 3 specifies the model: its formulation and solution method, the demand and supply representations, the financing layer, the policy representation, the scenario and verification design, and the limitations conditioning how its results are to be read. Chapter 4 reports the results against the four questions of §1.4. Chapter 5 interprets those results, states what the study does and does not establish, and sets out the work following from it. Chapter 6 states the findings the thesis establishes, restates the contribution and its scope, and identifies the priorities among the items Chapter 5 sets out.

Four appendices support the text. Appendix A records the provenance of every parameter, classified and sourced. Appendix B reproduces the limitations register at the state of the frozen results. Appendix C reproduces the entries resolved during the study. Appendix D records the fixed constants and the reproducibility arrangements.

## 1. Demand: base and growth

**SETTLED**

### 1.1 Base year

The base-year demand level is **37.09 TWh**, gross generation at the busbar
[SOURCED: NERC 2024, 37,093.70 GWh]. The metering point is declared as gross
generation sent out because that is the natural boundary for a generation
capacity-expansion model: capacity serves generation, and the NDC emissions caps
account against grid output.

**Correction.** The base was previously 23.08 TWh, derived as NBS Q1 2024 served
energy (5.770 TWh) annualised as Q1×4. That figure is *collected* energy — a
revenue quantity at the bottom of the NERC loss ladder (37.09 gross → −7% TLF →
34.50 delivered → −36.4% ATC&C → 21.97 collected). It excludes electricity
consumed but stolen, unmetered or unpaid, all of which is real demand of real
users. Using it understated system scale by roughly 40%.

The Q1×4 annualisation is separately unsound, ignoring seasonality, and is not
used anywhere in the current model.

This correction is the origin of the general audit question applied throughout
the model: *was this number generated by the system working, or by the system
failing?* Section 1.2 is a second instance of the same error class.

### 1.2 Why historical generation growth cannot source demand growth

The original specification applied a single growth rate of 4%/yr, of unknown
provenance. The obvious replacement — calibrating to historical Nigerian
generation growth — was considered and **rejected as circular**.

Nigerian grid generation is a *supply* series. It has been flat because gas
supply, wheeling capability and the sector's payment chain held it flat, and
those are precisely the constraints this study exists to evaluate. Calibrating
exogenous demand to it assumes the constraint persists, and the model then
reports modest capacity requirements *because* demand was assumed not to grow.

In a capacity-expansion LP, exogenous demand is the **service obligation the
system is required to meet**, not a forecast of what will be delivered. Setting
that obligation equal to a supply-suppressed continuation of historical delivery
asks the model what the cheapest way is to keep the grid the size it already is.

The rejection is supported empirically rather than only in argument. Decomposing
observed generation growth against population [SOURCED: World Bank WDI
`SP.POP.TOTL`] and access [SOURCED: WDI `EG.ELC.ACCS.ZS`]:

| Basis | Generation | Denominator | Residual intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total population, 2010–2025 | +3.14%/yr | +2.39%/yr | **+0.73%/yr** |
| Total population, 2014–2024 | +1.53%/yr | +2.27%/yr | **−0.73%/yr** |
| Connected population, 2010–2024 | +2.63%/yr | +4.36%/yr | **−1.66%/yr** |

[All DERIVED: CAGRs computed from the cited series. The decomposition
`(1+g_gen) = (1+g_pop)(1+g_percap)` is exact.]

Between 67% and more than 100% of observed generation growth is demographic. Per
connected person, grid generation *fell* from approximately 327 to 258 kWh over
2010–2024, and is negative on every window tested. The series therefore carries
almost no information about consumption intensity.

**Direction of bias in the connected-population figure:** WDI access counts
off-grid and distributed renewable supply, not solely grid connections, so
"connected population" overstates *grid*-connected population and increasingly so
over time. The −1.66%/yr decline is therefore **exaggerated in magnitude, though
not in sign**. The true intensity trend lies between the two rows above — at or
below zero either way.

The same reasoning disqualifies income elasticities estimated on Nigerian or
sub-Saharan African time series: consumption response to income cannot be
observed where supply is rationed, so such estimates are **biased downward** and
are used only as a declared lower bound.

The methodological position is not novel to this study. NIRP 2024 (Table 14)
derives its national demand forecast bottom-up from population, GDP,
electrification rate and network-loss trajectories, explicitly rather than from
historical generation [SOURCED].

### 1.3 Growth arms and their derivation

Three arms, each adopted only where at least two independent sourcing routes
agree.

| Arm | Rate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| `constrained_continuation` | **3.14%** | [DERIVED] Ember 2010–2025 grid generation CAGR |
| `organic_central` | **3.9%** | [DERIVED] Route A 3.95%, Route B 3.67% |
| `organic_high` | **6.0%** | [DERIVED] Route A 6.30%, Route B 6.40%, NIRP low 5.9% |

**Route A — driver decomposition.** `g = ε·g_GDP + (1−ε)·g_pop`, where ε is the
long-run income elasticity of electricity demand.

- ε = **0.80**. Liddle & Hasanov (2023), *Environmental and Ecological
  Statistics*, p.827/p.829/p.843: economy-wide electricity demand (not
  total energy), 31-country middle-income panel, 1990–2019, income
  elasticity averaged 0.8 (stated range 0.7–0.9). Traced against the
  primary (register L54); supersedes an earlier 0.76–0.90 range with a
  0.83 midpoint, neither of which traced to this or any source. The
  model excludes SSA-estimated elasticities (0.60–0.76, unsourced
  here) as rationing-contaminated. Note the cited panel itself
  includes South Africa and Côte d'Ivoire (2 of 31), so this exclusion
  is partial.
- g_pop = **1.77%/yr**, the 2024–2045 average [DERIVED: UN WPP, 232.68m →
  336.66m]. Note this is the *average* over the horizon, not the terminal rate.
- g_GDP is a **declared assumption, not a forecast**. 4.5% central: above the
  post-2015 average, which contains two recessions, and materially below the
  oil-boom rate. It does not assume a commodity boom returns. Beyond roughly 2030
  no credible source forecasts Nigerian GDP, so this is stated as an assumption
  from that point.

The identity has a useful property: the coefficient on population is (1−ε) =
0.20, so **g is approximately ε × GDP growth**, and a 0.3pp change in the
population assumption moves g by about 0.06pp. The GDP assumption is four
times more load-bearing than the population assumption, which matters because
Nigeria's last census was 2006 and all population figures are contested
projections.

The specification is validated by reproducing NIRP: with ε = 1.19 (NIRP's own
implied value) and g_pop = 2.1% (NIRP's own assumption), the identity returns
7.70%, matching NIRP's stated total-demand CAGR exactly.

**Route B — per-capita convergence.** Nigeria's 2024 level is ~161 kWh/cap.
Convergence to a named comparator's level by 2045 yields g directly. Kenya
crossed 161 kWh/cap in approximately 2009, so Nigeria is roughly **15 years
behind Kenya** [DERIVED from Ember generation and WDI population]. Ghana cannot
serve as a target: Nigeria's 2024 level is below Ghana's *worst year since 2000*,
and convergence to Ghana's present level would require 9.24%/yr.

**Route C — NIRP 2024.** Total demand 59.6 → 328.6 TWh over 2022–45 = **7.70%**
[DERIVED from SOURCED endpoints]. This is **Tier 2 scope** — the base already
includes self-generation. NIRP's grid-only rate (11.0%) is organic growth *plus*
absorption of self-generation and must not be applied to a Tier 1 base; in this
architecture suppression closure is the gap between tiers, not a growth rate.
Held outside the arm registry as `demand_growth_benchmark()`.

**Naming.** `constrained_continuation` is a **constraint-persistence** arm, not
organic demand growth, and must never be presented as such. It represents the
counterfactual in which the sector's constraints hold. It clears the floor test
(g ≥ population growth) and implies 1.35%/yr per-capita growth — *above* the
0.73% actually observed — so even the pessimistic arm is mildly optimistic
against the record.

**Honest note on the outcome.** `organic_central` landed at 4.0%, essentially
where the unsourced placeholder was. The value barely moved; its status moved
entirely, from an assumption nobody could defend to a triangulated result with
three named routes and a declared GDP dependency.

### 1.4 Consequence: demand growth does not reach the emissions caps

The caps are constructed from the model's own baseline gas-to-power trajectory.
Rebuilding them from a `constrained_continuation` baseline yields caps
**byte-identical** to those from `organic_central`, because baseline gas reaches
its deliverability ceiling by 2026 and `Ebase` is set by the gas trajectory
rather than by demand.

The NDC caps are therefore robust to any revision of the demand arm, which
decouples the cap specification from the demand specification. The policy
corollary is uncomfortable and belongs in the discussion: because the cap is
anchored to a gas-constrained baseline, the trajectory is met substantially by
*remaining supply-constrained*.

## 2. Asset lifetimes and end-of-horizon salvage

**SETTLED** (specification); **PROVISIONAL** (interaction with the
capital-recovery formulation written elsewhere)

### 2.1 Why salvage is needed

The reporting horizon is shorter than every asset life, so without a terminal
value assets with 9–29 years of remaining service were fully expensed and system
cost was overstated. A 2045 solar plant was charged 100% of its capital cost for
one year of service from a 30-year asset.

Salvage is a straight-line residual credited at the horizon end and discounted:

```
salvage = DF[T_last] · Σ_t capex[t] · add[t] · max(0, life − (T_last − t + 1)) / life
```

Implemented identically in the annual and time-sliced builders. The two must
share a cost definition or the annual-versus-sliced resolution premium compares
different quantities.

### 2.2 Asset lifetimes

| Asset | Life | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Solar PV | **30 yr** | [SOURCED: IRENA (2026), *Renewable Power Generation Costs in 2025*] |
| Battery storage | **15 yr, unaugmented** | [SOURCED: NREL ATB, utility-scale battery] |
| Gas | **30 yr** | [SOURCED: NREL ATB, Gas-CC technical life] |

These are **technical service life** — how long the asset physically operates —
not capital recovery periods (a financial assumption used in LCOE, typically
shorter) and not performance warranties. The distinction is load-bearing: using a
recovery period would understate salvage.

The storage figure requires care. ATB's 30-year PV-plus-battery life assumes cell
replacement at year 15. This model carries no augmentation capital, so the
unaugmented 15-year figure is the correct one; adopting the augmented life would
credit residual value on an asset never paid to maintain.

### 2.3 Three accounting decisions

**Salvage enters the objective only, never the capital budget constraint.** The
public envelope constrains cash out the door. An investor cannot fund a 2045
build with residual value realised in 2045 or later. Netting salvage inside the
constraint would stretch the envelope by roughly a quarter for free and soften
the finding that public capital binds. The objective and the budget constraint
therefore use deliberately different cost definitions.

**Salvage is computed on raw capital cost for both financing arms.** The EaaS
`required_margin` is a financing premium, not asset value: a panel bought with
expensive capital is not worth more at resale. Applying the margin to salvage
would hand the EaaS arm a residual-value bonus proportional to its financing
cost, flattering the financing result in exactly the direction it must not be
flattered.

**Residual value accrues system-wide.** Coherent for a social-planner objective
but it blurs ownership, since in reality the EaaS investor holds it. Revisit when
the tranche formulation makes ownership explicit.

### 2.4 Declared simplifications and their direction

All three bias salvage **upward**, so the corrected system cost is a **lower
bound**:

1. Straight-line residual with no degradation. Solar output declines roughly
   0.5%/yr, so an old panel is worth less than straight-line implies.
2. No decommissioning or disposal cost.
3. A liquid residual value is assumed. In practice there is no secondary market
   for a 2045 Nigerian solar asset at book value; the real economic content is
   continued service beyond the horizon, which is a different claim.

A fourth, minor: the credit is discounted at `DF[T_last]` rather than
`DF[T_last+1]`, following the model's convention that year *t* occurs at time
*t*. This marginally understates discounting, so it too overstates salvage.

### 2.5 Correction to the original specification

The correction plan asserted that omitting salvage "drives pathological delay,
which the minimum-build floor patches." Both halves are wrong.

The balance constraint binds every year, so the model *cannot* delay past the
point of need — there is no all-delay solution available. And salvage makes late
builds *cheaper* in net terms, so it **strengthens** the delay incentive rather
than removing it. What omitting salvage actually caused was a **level** error,
not a timing error.

### 2.6 A boundary condition the model reports on itself

Storage life (15 years) is shorter than the model horizon, so a storage vintage
built early enough retires within the model while still being dispatched.
**Replacement capital IS now charged** for this: `m.cost_replacement_capex_npv`
(`pre_f1_overnight`, entering `system_cost_npv`) and
`m.cost_rebuild_capital_charge_npv` (`f1_tranche`, entering
`capital_charge_total_npv`) both sum a rebuild at every
`build_t + k*life <= T_last`. This was not always true — it has held
since Phase 2.6 (`pre_f1_overnight`) and since F2 (`f1_tranche`) — and
the post-solve guard described next was rewritten accordingly.

The solver wrapper carries a post-solve check, **rewritten to test the
actual invariant**: for every vintage whose remaining service span
exceeds its technical life, at least one rebuild year exists
(`optimize_model.py:1900-1925`; `docs/d6_storage_guard.md`). The OLD
guard asserted a defect (no replacement capex charged) that stopped
being true once the rebuild mechanism landed, and fired on every run as
a false positive; the new guard tests for the rebuild's presence instead
of assuming its absence.

This is not decoration. It caught an error during horizon selection (§3.2) and it
fires (correctly, now confirming rather than warning) on buffer-year storage
vintages under the current specification.

**Historical limitation, resolved.** Buffer-year storage vintages used to
retire in-model with no replacement capital charged, understating their
cost. **Replacement/rebuild capex is now charged** for these vintages
too, per the mechanism above. The buffer years are still never reported,
so this was never a reported-figure defect, but the "no replacement
capital" rationale itself no longer describes the code.

## 3. Planning horizon and build-rate constraints

**SETTLED** (specification); **PROVISIONAL** (whether the horizon remains
necessary once the capital-recovery formulation lands — see §6)

### 3.1 The artefact that determined the horizon

Salvage credits a final-year vintage at (life−1)/life of capital cost — 96.7% for
30-year solar — discounted at the same factor the capital is paid at. Net cost is
therefore approximately 3.3% of discounted capital, around $11.5/MWh, **below the
short-run marginal cost of gas**.

With an unsourced build-rate cap active this was invisible. When that cap was
removed (§3.3), the model exploited the arbitrage directly: final-year solar
additions rose from 1,635 MW to 17,861 MW, final-year gas generation fell from
24.67 to **zero**, cumulative solar rose from 25.3 to 39.3 GW, and **the objective
fell**. The model built 14 GW more capacity and paid less.

This is a horizon artefact, not an economic result. It is also the second
instance of a general lesson recorded in §6: an unsourced bound may be masking a
defect.

### 3.2 The horizon

**The model optimises over 2025–2054 and reports physical results to 2045.**

The fix is the horizon, not the salvage term. Salvage is unchanged — same
formula, every vintage. With the edge at 2054 a 2045 vintage serves 10 of its 30
years, so its salvage is 67% rather than 97% and its net cost is roughly a third
of capital: no longer cheaper than gas. 2045 becomes an ordinary interior year.

Horizon extension is the standard treatment for end-of-horizon effects in
capacity-expansion models. The edge cannot be removed — a 2045 vintage is not
fully depreciated until 2075 — only moved outside the reporting window.

**2054 rather than 2055** because at 2055 a 2025 solar vintage would serve 31
years against a 30-year life. The 31-year arithmetic still holds, but the
underlying rationale ("retiring in-model with no replacement capital
charged") **is now historical, not current**: under the present
formulation such a vintage past its life is rebuilt and its replacement
capex is charged (§2.6). The check described in §2.6 caught this
originally, before the rebuild mechanism existed.

**Buffer-year series are extrapolations, not sourced.** No published Nigerian
demand or gas deliverability projection extends past 2045. This is acceptable
precisely because those years are never reported; their only function is to hold
the terminal edge away from the results.

- Solar and storage capital costs are **held flat** past NREL ATB coverage.
  [ASSUMED, direction stated:] flat is the conservative choice — continuing the
  ATB decline would make buffer-year solar progressively cheaper and could pull
  build forward into the reporting window, the exact distortion the buffer
  exists to prevent.
- Gas deliverability extends the same functional forms.
- The solar deployment cap continues at its terminal growth rate. Clamping it
  flat would freeze deployment capability and could force unserved energy in the
  buffer, distorting decisions inside the window.
- **Emissions caps are held flat at the terminal in-window value through the
  buffer**, not released non-binding as this document previously stated, **for
  every scenario except `ndc3_conditional_decline`**, whose buffer years
  2046-2054 instead continue the linear decline
  (`scripts/00_build_emissions_cap.py:315-323`) — a deliberate departure
  from the flat-hold convention (C-12). NDC 3.0
  provides no target beyond 2035, so 2036–2045 is already an apportionment
  construction, and the buffer years extend that construction further rather than
  extrapolating past it. See `docs/methodology_genself_backstop.md` §4.5 for the
  full account — a non-binding buffer became an arbitrage sink once the
  self-generation backstop existed (defect D10), which is why this differs from
  the other buffer-year treatments listed above. Not re-derived here; that
  document is the canonical statement.
- Flat gas level-equivalents used by the gas shape-premium experiment are
  averaged over the **reporting window only**, so that comparison's basis is
  unchanged by the horizon.

### 3.3 Solar build-rate constraints

**Minimum build floor: removed.** A 100 MW/yr floor was justified as preventing
"pathological all-delay solutions that leave 2025–2030 with no solar." Testing
that justification: removing it moved 2025 from 100 to 0 MW and 2026 from 173.41
to 273.41 MW, with every other year identical and **cumulative build unchanged to
the megawatt**. The floor was not preventing delay; it was forcing 100 MW one
year early.

**At the epoch this floor-removal test was run** (before the min-build floor was
removed, and several corrections before the frozen state), the backcast arithmetic
showed why 2025 = 0 is correct: gas 24.57 + hydro 11.47 + existing solar 0.88 ≈
36.9 TWh against 37.09 TWh of demand — essentially no new solar required in the
base year. These component figures are historical and do not reproduce at the
frozen state (current backcast is -3.2%, not the -4.1% this arithmetic implies);
the qualitative conclusion they supported — a model that builds capacity it does
not need is the distorted one, so the floor was wrong to force 100 MW — does not
depend on the exact split and is unaffected.

**Maximum build rate: replaced.** A flat 2,000 MW/yr cap was unsourced and
binding in the terminal years. It was wrong in shape at both ends — permitting
2,000 MW in 2026, when the national plan judges roughly 240 MW achievable, and
capping the 2040s at 2,000 when the national plan builds 6.0–8.3 GW/yr.

The general point: **a constant annual rate against compounding demand must bind
eventually at any level.** The terminal build sitting exactly on the cap was
therefore an artefact of the constraint's *shape*, not evidence of a deployment
limit.

Replaced by a cumulative deployment trajectory taken from NIRP 2024 [DERIVED:
Annex F Table 19 solar generation converted to capacity using the Table 8
capacity factor, 19% declining to 18%]. The conversion validates: it reaches
61,898 MW at 2045 against NIRP's published headline of approximately 61 GW.

Applied to cumulative *additions* rather than total capacity, because NIRP's 2025
figure (180 MW) is below this model's existing solar baseline (500 MW) and a
total-capacity cap would be infeasible in the first year. **Direction of bias:**
this is mildly generous, since NIRP's total includes existing plant.

Three arms: `nirp_trajectory` (1.0×), `nirp_accelerated` (2.0× — [ASSUMED],
pending comparator national ramps), and `deployment_unconstrained` (no cap, as
the counterfactual isolating what the deployment limit costs).

**Coherence caveat.** NIRP builds almost no solar before 2032 *because* it
assumes gas recovers substantially by 2035; its solar schedule is downstream of
its gas assumption. Pairing `nirp_trajectory` with a constrained-gas regime is
therefore a stress corner rather than a central case. See §5.

### 3.4 Emissions cap construction

**SETTLED** (the two-layer construction); **additive sensitivity** (the
declining arm, plan 4.2)

The model's emissions cap is not a single sourced series; it is built in two
layers, and NDC 3.0 only supplies the first. NDC 3.0 states two numerical
targets — an economy-wide abatement of 168.2 MtCO2e by 2030 and 184.9 MtCO2e
by 2035 — and an energy-sector share of each. This model apportions a further
share of that energy-sector abatement to gas-fired power generation
specifically, in proportion to gas-power's own share of modelled
energy-sector emissions (plan 4.1). That apportionment rule is this
project's construction, not NDC 3.0's; NDC 3.0 says nothing about how much
of its energy-sector target the power sector alone should carry, let alone
gas-fired power within it. The result is two anchor points per cap
scenario — a 2030 cap and a 2035 cap — built from real NDC 3.0 numbers
through one stated, but unavoidably analytical, apportionment step.

Past 2035, NDC 3.0 gives no target at all. Every cap value from 2036 onward
— ten of the twenty-one years this model reports, plus the entire
2046–2054 buffer (§3.2 above, "Emissions caps are held flat...") — is
therefore a second-layer construction, stacked on the first. The original
construction was a single, largely unstated choice: hold the 2035 cap flat
through the rest of the horizon. Flat is not a neutral default. It encodes
an implicit assumption that climate ambition stops accumulating in 2035,
which understates the abatement a country actually committed to net-zero by
2060 would need to deliver across the back two-thirds of a thirty-year
power-sector planning horizon. The flat hold was silently doing policy work
it was never declared to be doing.

The `ndc3_conditional_decline` arm (plan 4.2) adds a second, declared
construction alongside the first rather than replacing it: a cap that
declines linearly from the `ndc3_conditional` 2035 anchor toward zero at
2060 — the year Nigeria has itself named as its net-zero target, making the
endpoint policy-anchored rather than analyst-chosen. The straight line
between the 2035 anchor and that endpoint is, in exactly the same sense as
the plan 4.1 apportionment rule, this project's own construction: no
Nigerian document specifies that the path between those two points is
linear rather than front- or back-loaded. It is reported as a sensitivity
arm precisely because of that — a shape assumption introduced three days
before a model freeze is evidence that a range exists, not a claim that
this particular range is correct. Reporting it as a bound alongside the
flat hold, rather than substituting one unstated assumption for one
declared one, is the more defensible position this close to submission. See
register **L24** for the declared direction of bias and the interaction
with the plan 4.1 apportionment rule.

## 4. Cost reporting basis

**SETTLED**

Because the model optimises over 2025–2054, its objective spans 30 years. A
30-year financial figure reported alongside 21-year capacity and emissions
figures is incoherent, so the two are separated explicitly.

| Quantity | Horizon | Salvage | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| `system_cost_npv` | 2025–2054 | included **under `pre_f1_overnight` only** — `m.salvage_npv` does not appear in the `f1_tranche` branch, which instead carries `m.capital_charge_total_npv` (the per-tranche capital charge, with its own truncation) | **Optimisation objective.** Drives decisions, comparable across arms. Not a headline |
| `npv_gross_capex_report_window_usd` | 2025–2045 | **excluded** | **Reported financing quantity** |

At the central configuration (`f1_tranche`), `system_cost_npv` therefore
carries no salvage term at all; truncation replaces it, per
`docs/methodology.md` §4.2. The "included" salvage row above describes
the legacy `pre_f1_overnight` regression path only.

Its components, including fixed and variable O&M, are enumerated in
`docs/methodology.md` §4.2 — not repeated here.

The reported metric is discounted **gross capital expenditure over the reporting
window**, capital only. Fuel and operating costs are paid from operating revenue,
not from mobilised capital, so they do not belong in a capital-mobilisation
figure. Salvage is excluded for the same reason it is excluded from the budget
constraint (§2.3): residual value is not money anyone can spend during the
window.

This is the quantity the research question is about — how much capital must be
mobilised between 2025 and 2045 — and it is consistent with how the capital
envelope is specified.

**Reporting rule:** whenever either figure is quoted, the horizon and the salvage
basis are named in the same sentence. Quoting one in one chapter and the other in
another, without stating which is which, is the failure mode this separation
exists to prevent.

## 5. Scenario coherence: a declared asymmetry

**OPEN** (the declaration is written; the quantitative cross-tab awaits the
genset backstop)

Demand arms are sourced from **unconstrained** drivers — population, GDP,
elasticity, per-capita convergence. Gas deliverability is anchored to **observed
constrained** throughput, on a constraint that is commercial and midstream rather
than geological.

The model therefore assumes the sector's constraints lift on the demand side and
persist on the supply side, and **that asymmetry is what generates the solar
residual**, and with it the storage build and the financing frontier.

This is a scenario design choice, not an error, and the argument for it is
structural. Demand is a *requirement*; gas deliverability is a *capability*.
Those are different objects, and pairing an unconstrained requirement with an
observed capability is the correct treatment of two quantities that differ in
kind. It is also the condition the thesis exists to analyse: Nigerian planning
has repeatedly set demand-side ambition that upstream delivery did not match.

But it must be **argued rather than inherited**, because the asymmetric cell is
also the region of the scenario space that maximises the financing gap — the
headline quantity the study measures. Along a coherent diagonal the extremes
partially cancel; off-diagonal they compound.

The declaration lives in `docs/scenario_coherence.md` with a cross-tab
classifying each demand-arm × gas-regime cell as coherent, asymmetric, plausible
or incoherent, and a reporting rule: incoherent cells appear as sensitivity
bounds only, never as central findings.

Two further instances of the same asymmetry are recorded there. The solar-build
axis (§3.3) is a third dimension the cross-tab does not yet cover. And GDP is
exogenous to electricity supply although supply constrains GDP in reality; that
feedback is declared rather than modelled, because endogenising it would add a
second unsourced parameter, dilute the contribution, and break the linear
formulation on which the verification apparatus depends. **Direction of bias:**
excluding high-GDP/high-shortfall cells removes demand, so the omission is
conservative with respect to the financing gap.

## 6. Verification protocol

**SETTLED**

The protocol is part of the method because several specification errors in this
model were invisible to internal consistency and were found only by procedure.

**Backcasting against observed output is the validation instrument**, not
internal consistency. Two compensating errors in the original specification were
undetectable until the model was compared to reality. First-year gas generation
against observed output is the standing test, and it must survive every change:
if a correction moves the base year, the correction is entering wrongly.

**Split renames from revaluations.** Rename with values frozen first — outputs
must be byte-identical, which is a free exact regression test across the entire
codebase. Change values in a second commit, so every difference is attributable.
Reversed, a rename error is permanently indistinguishable from a value effect.

**Design an internal control.** In one experiment, unchanged rows for two demand
arms sitting between changed rows for a third, in the same output file from the
same solve, is stronger evidence than any external comparison.

**Log every runner separately and check exit codes.** A solver reporting "optimal"
is not a pass; a run can succeed and then fail in post-processing. Six runners
were failing silently under output redirection for an extended period because no
logged sweep had been run.

**Set a materiality threshold before reading a sensitivity verdict.** A
sensitivity test that flags any nonzero deviation will report a sub-2% movement
as responsiveness.

**An unsourced bound may be masking a defect.** Both build-rate bounds concealed
artefacts at opposite ends of the horizon (§3.1, §3.3). When an unsourced
constraint is removed, expect the result to change in a way that looks wrong, and
check whether the constraint was load-bearing for a reason nobody documented.

**The audit question**, applied to every parameter: *was this number generated by
the system working, or by the system failing?* Four instances found: the
collected-energy demand base (§1.1), a peak-truncated load factor, the demand
growth rate (§1.2), and — still open — the gas deliverability anchor, which is
observed constrained throughput used as a capability parameter.

This is presented as rigour, not as a contribution. Suppressed demand and
constrained-versus-unconstrained forecasting are standard in developing-country
utility planning.

## 7. Open method decisions

**Whether the horizon extension remains necessary.** **The capital-recovery
(F1 tranche) formulation is the central configuration, not something
developed "in parallel"** — `f1_tranche` is `capital_treatment_case`'s
central arm, and it removes the terminal-value arbitrage structurally
through annuity truncation in place of salvage (`methodology.md` §4.2).
Whether the horizon buffer is still needed for its original purpose,
given that truncation now does the same job at the central
configuration, remains open — the reporting-horizon rule it established
is being relied upon independently regardless. This needs resolving
rather than leaving both mechanisms in place unexamined.

**Whether capacity responds to cost.** Sensitivity testing under the
pre-existing financing formulation found the capacity trajectory invariant across
every plausible asset lifetime, in both the annual and sliced models, under cost
swings of 12–22%. Work elsewhere reports that capital recovery *does* move
technology composition. The two are probably reconcilable — asset lifetime is a
weak lever and cost of capital a strong one — but the sensitivity has not been
re-run under the new formulation and the earlier observation should not be cited
until it has.

**The `nirp_accelerated` multiplier** (2.0×) is an analyst assumption. Candidate
anchors are comparator national deployment ramps.

**Distributed solar capital cost and capacity factor — sourced, NOT
implemented, unmeasured (register L25, corrected).** The distributed
capex premium, capacity-factor cut, and seasonal profile (DIST-5) are
sourced from NREL ATB and Global Solar Atlas in
`docs/shape_factors_and_dist5.md`, but no builder or scenario code wires
them in. Register L25 previously reported this item as resolved with a
measured effect of under 0.03%; that measurement was never computed by
any solve and has been withdrawn (methodology claims ledger C-10). L16
is the accurate entry: DIST-5 and its counterpart DIST-2 (loss
advantage, favours EaaS) are both unimplemented and, because they
oppose, their joint effect has no established direction. See register
L25 for the full account.

**Gas fleet efficiency is a fixed parameter.** Measured fleet efficiency is low,
and improving it would release materially more electricity from the same fuel
with no new infrastructure. It is plausibly the cheapest abatement option
available in this system and the model cannot represent it. Adopted as a
scheduled addition; not yet implemented.

**The solar-build axis is missing from the coherence cross-tab** (§5).

**λ is a scenario parameter, not a model output.** An earlier design claim that
the tiered demand architecture makes the suppression ratio a derived statistic
was **incorrect**: with one organic growth rate applied to three demand scopes,
λ(t) = λ(0) by construction. It becomes endogenous only once the model can
economically decline to serve, which is the function of the backstop specified
elsewhere. Central value 0.51–0.57 [DERIVED from NIRP 2024 Tables 2 and 15 and
§4.2].
